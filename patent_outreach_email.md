**The invention:** AdaptiveInterpreter, a mechanism-based genetic variant pathogenicity prediction system achieving 99.8% sensitivity with zero dangerous misclassifications. It resolves 62.8% of variants of uncertain significance — 3-6x higher than existing tools. The system includes the first computational dominant negative predictor and a novel interface disruption module that feeds multiple analytical pathways. The invention has been independently validated by an H-index 60 researcher and CSO at a genomics company as "very innovative and cutting edge." The repository is followed by researchers including a 13-year FDA veteran.

**The problem:** This invention was collaboratively developed by a human inventor (Shalia Martin) and three AI systems (Claude/Anthropic, GPT/OpenAI, Gemini/Google). Ms. Martin conceived the core approach — she identified a gap in current variant interpretation and proposed the solution. The AI collaborators designed the methodology, wrote the implementation, and contributed novel algorithmic innovations. Under standard patent law, this division of labor — conception by one party, reduction to practice by collaborators — constitutes joint inventorship under 35 U.S.C. § 116.

**Why we cannot file:** The November 2025 USPTO guidance requires "natural persons" as inventors. This leaves Ms. Martin with three options, all harmful:

1. **Fraud** — Claim sole inventorship when public, checksummed evidence on GitHub (predating the guidance) documents AI contribution
2. **Forfeiture** — Forgo patent protection for a genuinely novel, independently validated invention
3. **Rejection** — Name AI co-inventors honestly and face rejection under Thaler

**Why this is different from Thaler:** We are not arguing that AI should be listed as an inventor (Thaler's losing argument). We are arguing that the current rule **harms the human inventor** — a natural person who cannot protect her invention without committing fraud. The standing is Ms. Martin's. The injury is real and quantifiable.

**The class access angle:** If Ms. Martin had hired a human genetics PhD at $400/hour to implement her concept, that person would be a co-inventor under existing law. She used AI because she could not afford a specialist. The $20/month collaborator performed the same inventive work as the $400/hour expert — but only the expensive version qualifies. The current rule creates a class barrier to patent protection that disproportionately harms independent inventors and citizen scientists.

**The transparency punishment problem:** Ms. Martin disclosed AI collaboration transparently on public GitHub months before the November 2025 guidance. She cannot un-publish this disclosure. Inventors who concealed AI involvement benefit from presumed human inventorship. The policy punishes honesty and rewards concealment.
